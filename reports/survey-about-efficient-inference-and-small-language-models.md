# Small Language Models and Efficient Inference: A Comprehensive Survey

## TL;DR

- Small language models (SLMs) with 100M–5B parameters have emerged as a practical alternative to large LLMs for on-device deployment, offering significant efficiency gains while maintaining competitive performance on many tasks [1][2].
- Quantization techniques like AWQ and GPTQ at INT4 achieve ~4× memory reduction with minimal accuracy loss, and the Marlin kernel can make quantized inference up to 10.9× faster than FP16 baselines [3][4][5].
- Knowledge distillation enables SLMs to inherit capabilities from larger teachers through multiple mechanisms including reverse KL divergence (MiniLLM), intermediate-layer matching (TinyBERT), and multi-step cascaded transfer [6][7][8].
- Pruning and sparse architectures — from structured N:M sparsity to mixture-of-experts routing — reduce FLOPs by 14–60% while preserving accuracy, though hardware-aware co-design is essential to realize theoretical speedups [2][9][10].
- Recent advances (2024–2025) show Phi-4-reasoning (14B) surpassing DeepSeek-R1 (671B) on AIME 2025, and edge-deployment frameworks are maturing alongside KV-cache compression and speculative decoding innovations [11][12].

## Background

The field of small language models and efficient inference has been shaped by evolving understanding of scaling laws. Kaplan et al. established that cross-entropy loss follows a power-law relationship with model size and dataset size, suggesting that larger models are more sample-efficient and optimal compute allocation favors bigger models (N ∝ C^0.73) [13]. However, the Chinchilla study demonstrated that current LLMs were significantly undertrained, finding that model size and training tokens should scale equally (Nopt ∝ C^0.50, Dopt ∝ C^0.50), and that Chinchilla (70B parameters, 1.4T tokens) outperformed much larger contemporaries like Gopher (280B) and GPT-3 (175B) by over 7% on MMLU [13]. This paradigm shift toward data-centric training has been particularly important for SLMs, which tend to be "over-trained" relative to Chinchilla's predictions because device memory and compute constraints limit deployment rather than training FLOPs [1].

Small language models are typically defined as decoder-only transformer models in the 100M–5B parameter range [1][2]. The upper bound of 5B reflects the observation that even 7B models remain primarily cloud-deployed as of late 2024, while sub-5B models can run on smartphones, tablets, wearables, and IoT devices [1]. The proliferation of SLMs accelerated dramatically since the end of 2023, with over 70 state-of-the-art open-source SLMs documented in comprehensive surveys [1][2]. These models enable privacy-preserving on-device inference, reduced energy consumption, lower latency, and elimination of cloud-dependency — critical advantages for real-world applications where connectivity may be unreliable or data sensitivity is high [14][15].

Efficient inference encompasses a broad set of techniques spanning model compression (quantization, pruning, distillation), architectural optimization (sparse MoE, dynamic computation), and runtime improvements (KV-cache management, speculative decoding). The taxonomy proposed by Nguyen et al. organizes these methods along two axes: preprocessing/training/post-processing stages, and the specific constraint being optimized (inference compute, latency, memory, throughput, or storage) [2].

## Quantization Techniques

Quantization reduces the numerical precision of model weights and activations, providing substantial memory and compute savings. Post-training quantization (PTQ) approaches apply quantization after full training without retraining, while quantization-aware training (QAT) simulates quantization effects during training using fake quantization operations, generally achieving better accuracy especially at lower bit-widths [16].

SmoothQuant pioneered 8-bit weight and 8-bit activation (W8A8) quantization for LLMs by migrating quantization difficulty from outlier-prone activations to easily quantizable weights through a mathematically equivalent per-channel scaling transformation [17]. This training-free method achieves up to 1.56× speedup and 2× memory reduction on PyTorch with negligible accuracy loss, enabling serving of OPT-175B using half the GPUs compared to FP16 [17].

At 4-bit precision, AWQ (Activation-aware Weight Quantization) and GPTQ (Generative Pre-trained Transformer Quantization) dominate the landscape. AWQ identifies important weights via activation patterns and scales them before quantization, completing in 5–15 minutes for 7B models. GPTQ uses second-order Hessian information layer-by-layer to compensate for rounding errors, requiring 30–60 minutes for 7B models [4]. Benchmarks consistently show AWQ slightly outperforming GPTQ on accuracy metrics: ~5.40 vs ~5.50 perplexity on WikiText-2, ~83% vs ~81–82% on MMLU, and ~70% vs ~66–67% on HumanEval for Vicuna-7B [18]. However, GPTQ supports more extreme compression down to 2–3 bits, while AWQ is optimized specifically for INT4 [4][18].

The choice of dequantization kernel matters enormously. With the Marlin kernel, quantized inference can be up to 10.9× faster than FP16 baselines, with AWQ achieving notably higher throughput than GPTQ at INT4 precision [5]. Without Marlin, standard GPTQ (~276 tok/s) is faster than standard AWQ (~67 tok/s), demonstrating that kernel implementation can matter more than the quantization algorithm itself [5]. For code generation (HumanEval Pass@1), all quantized variants except GPTQ maintain ~51.83% versus the FP16 baseline of 56.1%, while GPTQ drops to 46.34% [5].

QLoRA introduced NormalFloat (NF4), an information-theoretically optimal data type for normally distributed weights, combined with double quantization and paged optimizers to enable fine-tuning a 65B-parameter model on a single 48GB GPU [3]. NF4 maintains accuracy within 0.1% of full precision on downstream tasks while achieving 4× memory reduction [19]. Recent work on fast NF4 dequantization kernels leverages shared memory optimization to achieve 2.0–2.2× kernel speedup across Gemma 27B, Qwen3 32B, and Llama3.3 70B, addressing the fact that dequantization accounts for roughly 30–40% of end-to-end latency in Qwen3-32B [19].

INT4 quantization of both weights and activations (W4A4) halves model size compared to INT8 but faces specific accuracy challenges in decoder-only models unlike encoder-only and encoder-decoder architectures [20]. At the extreme, GGUF formats (Q4_K_M) provide the most effective prefill improvements (~50% latency reduction) and decode-stage gains of 17–75% [1].

## Knowledge Distillation

Knowledge distillation transfers knowledge from large teacher models to compact students through multiple complementary mechanisms. Approaches are broadly categorized as white-box (teacher internal states available) or black-box (only API outputs accessible) [7].

The TinyBERT framework established foundational techniques for Transformer-specific distillation, introducing three types of loss functions: embedding layer output matching, hidden-state and attention-matrix distillation, and prediction-layer logit matching [6]. Attention-based distillation captures linguistic knowledge such as syntax and coreference encoded in attention weights. TinyBERT4 achieves over 96.8% of BERT-Base performance on GLUE with only ~13.3% of parameters and ~10.6% inference time [6]. MiniLMv2 extended this by transferring multi-head self-attention relations (scaled dot-products of query-key-value pairs) as distillation signals, eliminating restrictions on student head counts and demonstrating that upper-middle layers (e.g., layer 21 of BERT-Large) provide optimal signal transfer [8].

For autoregressive LLM distillation, MiniLLM replaced standard forward KL divergence with reverse KL divergence (KL[q||p]), preventing the student from overestimating low-probability regions of the teacher distribution and producing mode-seeking behavior that focuses on major modes rather than long-tail variants — critical for truthfulness and reliability [21]. This approach derives an on-policy optimization using policy gradients with single-step decomposition, teacher-mixed sampling, and length normalization for stable training.

Multi-step distillation bridges capacity gaps more effectively than direct one-step transfer. A configuration cascading 1.5B → 760M → 340M reduces perplexity from 24.77 to 17.55, substantially approaching the original teacher's predictive confidence of 21.94 [8]. The optimal teacher assistant lies near the geometric midpoint between teacher and student capacity; overly deep chains dilute useful signal and introduce noise [8]. Reverse KL objectives prevent overfitting to low-probability teacher outputs, reducing error propagation at each step [8].

Recent findings challenge conventional assumptions about distillation quality. Research on chain-of-thought reasoning distillation reveals non-monotonic relationships: stronger teacher models do NOT always produce better students, and diversity and complexity in CoT supervision can outweigh pure accuracy [8]. Human-annotated CoTs underperform LLM-generated CoTs on mathematical tasks, and the Matthew Effect applies — stronger student models achieve greater gains from CoT distillation due to wider zones of proximal development [8]. CoT format significantly impacts LLMs but has minimal effect on SLMs, likely due to their reliance on supervised fine-tuning rather than pretraining preferences [8].

Advantage-Guided Distillation for Preference Alignment combines dual-constrained knowledge distillation with advantage guidance to improve alignment and performance of SLMs guided by teacher LLMs [10]. CMoE provides a novel framework for converting dense LLMs to MoE models using expert grouping and differentiable routing, achieving high performance with minimal resources [22].

## Pruning and Sparse Architectures

Pruning techniques span unstructured removal of individual weights, structured removal of parameter groups, and architectural designs that inherently activate fewer parameters per token.

Unstructured magnitude-based pruning removes individual weights but produces irregular sparse matrices difficult to support on common hardware [2]. More recent gradient-free approaches like Wanda evaluate feature map saliency using weight magnitude alone, while MINI-LLM introduces Feature Map Sensitivity (FMS) scores integrating weight magnitude, activation, and estimated zeroth-order gradients via SPSA approximation [7]. MINI-LLM consistently surpasses gradient-free methods and matches backpropagation-based methods while using similar GPU memory as gradient-free approaches, reducing total inference time to 58% at 50% pruning ratio for LLaMA-7B [7].

Structured pruning removes groups of consecutive parameters (rows, columns, k×k blocks) enabling significant speedup without special hardware requirements [2]. FLOP (Factorized Low-rank Pruning) parameterizes each weight matrix as W = PQ and prunes rank-1 components during training, achieving 50% compression with only 0.8 perplexity loss on word-level language models and over 2× speedup during both training and inference [2]. LLM-Pruner introduces dependency detection algorithms identifying interdependent structures within LLMs, then estimates group importance using first-order gradient and approximated Hessian information, pruning up to ~60% of LLaMA parameters while achieving equivalent perplexity — compared to magnitude-dependent algorithms that collapse at ~20% [20].

N:M structured sparsity, particularly 2:4 patterns supported by NVIDIA Ampere tensor cores, offers a sweet spot between sparsity density and hardware compatibility [16]. Training recipes using decaying pruning masks (floating-point values decaying from 1→0 instead of binary 1/0) enable gradients of pruned weights to flow through networks more robustly. At 1:16 sparsification, model size reduces by 62% and inference FLOPS reduce by 60%, achieving state-of-the-art quality comparable to unstructured sparsity on translation tasks [16].

Mixture-of-experts architectures achieve efficient scaling by activating only k experts per token. ReMoE replaces conventional TopK+Softmax routing with ReLU routing as a drop-in replacement, where ReLU's naturally non-negative outputs represent expert weights including zeros for inactive experts [8]. Fully differentiable continuous routing enables dynamic allocation of computation across tokens and layers, with adaptive load-balancing L1 regularization targeting desired sparsity levels. Fine-grained ReMoE with G=32 and G=64 reaches Dense×8 performance upper bounds with significantly fewer FLOPs [8].

AdaMOE introduces null experts (empty operations requiring zero FLOPs) into MoE for token-adaptive routing, allowing different tokens to select varying numbers of experts. Applied to Mixtral-8x7B fine-tuning, it reduces FLOPs by 14.5% while increasing accuracy by 1.69% on ARC-C and achieving average 15.21% FLOPs reduction across datasets [9]. SERE addresses batch decoding conflicts with expert sparsity by dynamically re-routing tokens based on similarity patterns, achieving up to 2.0× speedup with minimal quality degradation [23].

Hardware implications are critical: unstructured pruning struggles with metadata overhead at moderate sparsity, while N:M structured sparsity requires co-designed CUDA kernels (MD-SpMM, SpInfer) to translate arithmetic savings into real end-to-end latency improvements on Tensor Core GPUs [16].

## Edge Deployment and Hardware Acceleration

Deploying SLMs on edge devices requires careful consideration of hardware-specific optimizations. Memory footprint scales from 275MB to 2456MB for 0.1B–3B models, with vocabulary size critically impacting memory requirements — Bloom-560M with 250K vocab requires 3.5× more memory than OpenELM-1.1B with 32K vocab [19]. Mul_mat_vec operations constitute over 70% of end-to-end inference time, making quantization more effective in the bandwidth-bound decode stage than the compute-bound prefill stage [19].

The prefill stage dominates on-device LLM inference due to long-context processing needs for personalization, while the decode stage is linearly memory-bound [19]. This asymmetry suggests different optimization strategies for each phase: aggressive quantization for decode, and potentially speculative decoding or KV-cache compression for prefill.

Comprehensive evaluations of commercial CPUs (Intel, ARM), GPUs (NVIDIA), and NPUs (RaiderChip) reveal that specialized backends outperform general-purpose CPUs, with NPUs achieving highest performance by wide margin [21]. Low-power ARM processors deliver competitive results when considering energy usage; Energy-Delay Product (EDP) metrics highlight NPUs as the dominant architecture for edge workloads [21]. Modern CPUs increasingly incorporate dedicated features targeting language-model workloads, narrowing the gap with specialized hardware [21].

Edge deployment frameworks including ExecuTorch, ONNX Runtime, and TensorRT-Edge-LLM have matured significantly, providing production-ready toolchains for deploying SLMs across diverse hardware platforms [11]. Phi Silica, optimized for Copilot+ PC NPUs with ONNX support, provides preloaded memory, fast time-to-first-token, and power-efficient token throughput for offline use in Windows experiences [11].

## Trends and Open Problems

The period 2024–2025 has witnessed dramatic capability gains in SLMs driven by synthetic data curation and reinforcement learning. Microsoft's Phi-4-reasoning (14B parameters) trained on distilled demonstrations from OpenAI o3-mini now rivals and exceeds much larger models including DeepSeek-R1 (671B) on AIME 2025 [12]. Phi-4-reasoning-plus uses reinforcement learning to generate longer reasoning traces for higher accuracy, and parallel test-time scaling nearly saturates AIME 2025 performance, surpassing pass@1 of the teacher model o3-mini [12]. Phi-4-mini-reasoning (3.8B) outperforms models twice its size and is comparable to OpenAI o1-mini on Math-500 and GPQA Diamond [11]. Reasoning capabilities generalize to out-of-domain tasks (3SAT, TSP, calendar planning) without explicit training, and coding scores reached o1-mini level on LiveCodeBench despite limited coding data [12].

Speculative decoding has evolved from simple draft-then-verify pipelines to sophisticated architectures. Wide/shallow draft models achieve up to 111% higher throughput than existing drafts, with pruned wide draft models reducing KV-cache by 37% and enabling larger batch sizes [15]. QuantSpec introduces self-speculative decoding with hierarchical 4-bit quantized KV cache and 4-bit quantized weights, maintaining >90% acceptance rates and providing consistent end-to-end speedups up to ~2.5× for long-context inference [15]. Cacheback demonstrates speculative decoding with nothing but cache reuse, eliminating the need for separate draft models entirely [15].

KV-cache compression remains a critical frontier. SmallKV leverages attention matrix similarity between LLMs of different scales, using smaller models to approximate attention of marginal tokens in larger models, achieving 1.75×–2.56× higher throughput than baseline methods and compatible with Flash Attention [15].

Several open problems persist. Optimal data scaling methods for SLMs remain unresolved, with current models appearing "over-trained" relative to classical scaling law predictions [1]. Long-context handling continues to challenge edge deployment, where KV-cache memory becomes the primary bottleneck [15]. The debate about whether tiny models can match larger ones through better training data, superior prompting, or architectural innovation remains active [14][15]. Hardware-software co-design for emerging accelerators (NPUs, TPUs) requires further investigation to fully exploit the potential of sparse and quantized models [21]. Finally, the question of how to best combine multiple compression techniques — quantization plus pruning plus distillation — without compounding accuracy losses represents an important direction for future research [16][8].
